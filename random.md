A major open question in AI research is whether large language models (LLMs) like those behind ChatGPT actually reason through problems computationally, or whether their internals work in ways fundamentally unlike human thinking. Researchers in a growing field called mechanistic interpretability have been mapping out "circuits" inside these models, which are small subnetworks of components responsible for specific behaviors like retrieving facts or recognizing patterns. But a deeper question extends these works: is the model actually utilizing step by step reasoning (hence using these circuits) or condensing its mental model differently? To investigate this, we turned to mental math — arithmetic problems like 42 + 20 − 15 that a model can solve in a single step without showing any work. Mental math is an ideal test case because when humans solve such problems, we naturally decompose them: first compute 42 + 20 = 62, then subtract 15 to get 47. If LLMs reason similarly, we would expect to find intermediate results being computed at different positions in the sequence and combined across layers. What we found instead tells a very different story.
Using two novel techniques developed as part of this research (Context-Aware Mean Ablation (CAMA) and Attention-Based Peeking (ABP)) we systematically stripped away parts of the model's internal computation to find the minimum structure that still produces correct answers. CAMA works by replacing each token's representation with an averaged version that preserves general understanding (like "this is an addition problem") while erasing knowledge of the specific numbers involved. ABP controls which tokens are allowed to share information with each other at each layer. Together, these tools let us carefully dismantle the model's wiring and observe what breaks.
The central finding is what we call the "All-for-One" (AF1) subgraph: a three-stage computational pattern where the model first spends roughly half its layers doing general preparation without any tokens sharing specific information, then briefly transfers all relevant information to the final token in just two layers, and finally lets that single token finish the calculation on its own. This means that out of 32 layers of computation and hundreds of possible communication pathways, only a tiny fraction (or a "short burst" of information flow to one position) is doing the real work. Rather than distributing computation across the sequence the way a human would — solving sub-problems at intermediate steps — the model funnels everything to a single position and solves the entire problem there at once. Notably, this pattern held across multiple models and a wide variety of arithmetic tasks, and the same small handful of attention heads were responsible for transferring operand information in every case.
This work contributes to the growing field of mechanistic interpretability by not only mapping out a circuit but also revealing what that circuit implies about how LLMs think. The discovery that these models concentrate all input-specific arithmetic computation at a single token position challenges prior assumptions that they reason compositionally the way humans do, even on tasks simple enough that compositional reasoning would seem natural. This has broader implications: if LLMs don't decompose problems into sub-steps even when the task clearly invites it, this may reflect a fundamental consequence of how they are trained — optimizing to predict one token at a time, with no incentive to store useful intermediate results elsewhere in the sequence. Beyond these findings, the CAMA and ABP techniques introduced here offer new general-purpose tools for investigating how information flows inside any large language model, with potential applications to understanding more complex reasoning behaviors.
